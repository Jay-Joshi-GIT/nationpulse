An Explainable Model versus a Black-Box LLM for Scoring Economies and Planning Growth: A Neutral Head-to-Head Comparison

Jay Prafulkumar Joshi
Roll Number: 26210062
ES 678
Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar

Abstract

Can a local large language model (LLM) replace a small explainable model for judging a country's economy and suggesting how to improve it? We compare XGBoost with SHAP explanations against Mistral 7B on 52 countries, using seven World Bank and IMF indicators for 2014–2023. Neither judges the other: both are checked against the real GDP-based score, historically achieved rates of change, standard economic direction, and computing cost. Scored on countries it never saw, XGBoost is 9.1 points off on a 0–100 scale against the LLM's 17.8, and a plain linear regression (13.3) also beats the LLM. The LLM compresses scores towards the middle, overrating low-income countries by 34 points, and changes its answer by 14 points when reworded. Its growth plans always move indicators in the textbook direction, while XGBoost's own plans break that direction in 30 of 51 countries. The LLM uses about 2,400 times more energy.

1  Problem and Motivation

LLMs are increasingly asked for economic judgements and policy advice, yet their reasoning cannot be inspected. Explainable models are transparent but narrow. We ask: on which aspects of scoring an economy and planning its growth does each kind of system do better? Each data row is one country in one year, described by seven indicators. The target is an economic score: the log of GDP per capita, min–max scaled to 0–100. A score is produced from the same year's indicators (we score 2023), and a strategy asks how the indicators should change to raise the score by 10 points within 5 years. The data supports claims about association between indicators and income, not about which policy causes growth.

2  Data and Preparation

Sources. World Bank World Development Indicators [1] (CC BY 4.0) give GDP per capita, inflation, unemployment, foreign direct investment, trade openness, life expectancy and education spending, compiled from the IMF, ILO, UN Population Division, UNESCO and national statistics. The World Bank lacked government debt for 21 of our first 41 countries, so general government gross debt comes from the IMF World Economic Outlook [2]. The sample is 52 countries in all four World Bank income groups (4 low, 14 lower-middle, 19 upper-middle, 15 high), 2014–2023: 520 rows. Education data is CC BY-SA; IMF data needs permission for commercial reuse.

Quality checks and preparation. The wbgapi client failed in October 2026, so data was fetched from the basic API, and one mistyped indicator code was corrected. Missing values were filled within each country only (interpolation, then nearest year), never across countries, so no information passes between training and test countries. Cells still missing (trade openness for Nigeria and Jordan, education spending for Egypt) are left empty; XGBoost handles them natively. The baseline regression fills gaps with medians computed on its training countries only. One transformation uses all rows: the minimum and maximum of log GDP that fix the 0–100 scale. It is a fixed rescaling of the target, applied identically to both systems.

3  Methods

Target. For country \(i\) with GDP per capita \(g_i\): \(s_i = 100\,(\log g_i - m)/(M - m)\), where \(m, M\) are the smallest and largest \(\log g\) in the data.

Baselines. (a) the average score of the training countries; (b) linear regression on the seven indicators.

System 1: XGBoost + SHAP. Gradient-boosted trees [3] predict \(\hat s(x)=\sum_{k} f_k(x)\), minimising \(\sum_i (s_i-\hat s_i)^2+\sum_k(\gamma T_k+\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\lVert w_k\rVert^2)\), where \(T_k\) and \(w_k\) are tree \(k\)'s leaf count and leaf weights (200 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.05, fixed in advance). TreeSHAP [4,5] splits each prediction exactly as \(\hat s(x)=\phi_0+\sum_{j=1}^{7}\phi_j(x)\), where \(\phi_j\) is indicator \(j\)'s contribution. A conformal interval [6,7] gives a 95% range.

System 2: LLM. Mistral 7B Instruct [8], run locally through Ollama at temperature 0 with JSON output, receives the same seven numbers and returns a score and a weight per indicator. Example values in the prompt anchored the model (scores clustered near the example's 50), so the prompt uses placeholders instead.

Strategies. For a target of +10 points in \(T=5\) years, System 1 runs a greedy search: each round it moves one indicator by 10–100% of its limit \(T L_j\), keeping the move with the largest predicted gain per step. Here \(L_j\) is the 90th percentile across countries of \(|x_{j,2023}-x_{j,2014}|/9\), the pace the fastest 10% of countries achieved. It is run with and without a rule allowing only the economically recommended direction \(d_j\in\{-1,+1\}\) (e.g. lower unemployment, inflation no lower than 2%); the unrestricted plan shows the model's own preferences. The LLM is given the score, target and indicators and returns target values, priorities and reasons.

4  Evaluation Design

The referee is never one of the competitors. Scoring is compared with the real score \(s_i\). Because the LLM never sees \(s_i\), XGBoost and the baselines are evaluated leave-one-country-out: each country is scored by a model trained on the other 51, with all its years held out together. No hyperparameters were tuned, so no test information enters model choice. Metrics: mean absolute error \(\tfrac1n\sum_i|\hat s_i-s_i|\); Spearman rank correlation with \(s_i\); group bias \(b_g=\operatorname{mean}_{i\in g}(\hat s_i-s_i)\) by income level and region, where fairness is judged by the worst group; consistency (score change when the question is reworded) and framing (numbers written as sentences). For the 95% range, models are trained on 70% of countries and the margin \(\hat q\) is the \(\lceil 0.95(n+1)\rceil/n\) quantile of absolute errors on the remaining 30%. Strategies cannot be judged on whether they reach the target, because only a model could say so and the model is a competitor. They are judged on realism (share of changed indicators with \(|\Delta_j|\le T L_j\) and inside the observed range) and direction (share of changes with \(\operatorname{sign}\Delta_j=d_j\)). Cost is estimated as run time \(\times\) the CPU's 15 W rating, converted with India's grid factor of 0.727 kg CO\(_2\)/kWh [9] and 3.5 L water/kWh.

Scoring. XGBoost wins on accuracy, ranking, fairness and consistency (Table 1). The LLM is better than guessing the average but worse than a linear regression. Figure 1 shows its main failure: every score lies between 35 and 75, so poor countries are overrated (Tanzania 60 vs. real 20; Mozambique 45 vs. 7) and rich ones underrated (Italy 55 vs. 86). Its error by income group runs from +34 (low) to −20 (high), against +8 to −4 for XGBoost. Rewording the question changed Argentina's score from 45 to 25.5, and writing the numbers as sentences moved scores by 8.4 points on average. XGBoost's failures are different: it cannot predict outside its training range (United States 73 vs. 100), and an indicator unlike any training country misleads it (Argentina's 133% inflation, predicted 95 vs. 66). South Africa's 32% unemployment pulls its score to 22 vs. 50. SHAP and the LLM's stated weights rank the indicators very differently (mean Spearman −0.19); without a true attribution, neither can be called right.

Strategies. The LLM always proposes textbook directions (100%), changes all seven indicators, and gives reasons, but only 65% of its changes are realistic, and only 36% for low-income countries, mostly life expectancy (42 unrealistic proposals) and education spending (36). XGBoost's plans are realistic by construction and use two indicators on average. Left to its own choice, however, it moves an indicator against economics in 30 of 51 countries, mostly by raising government debt (15) or cutting trade openness (11). Rich countries carry high debt in the data, so the model treats debt as a route to growth. For high-income countries its plans rarely reach the target (1 of 14), because trees cannot predict above the highest score seen. Cost. Both jobs for 52 countries took XGBoost 4.1 s (0.017 Wh) and the LLM 163 min (40.8 Wh, 29.7 g CO\(_2\), 143 ml water): about 2,400 times more.

6  Graduate Depth Analysis: How Much Does a Fair Evaluation Change the Answer?

Leakage ablation. Evaluated on countries it was trained on, XGBoost's MAE is 3.1 and its Spearman 0.98; scored on unseen countries they are 9.1 and 0.87. The in-sample error is three times too optimistic, and comparing it with the LLM would have exaggerated the gap from 8.7 to 14.7 points. XGBoost's remaining lead over the linear baseline (9.1 vs. 13.3) shows that a non-linear model adds accuracy, though not for countries outside the training range.

Uncertainty. The conformal margin is \(\pm22.9\) points: a 95% range about 46 points wide, which held the real score for all 16 calibration countries. Raising the confidence level from 95% to 99% widens it only to \(\pm26.5\) (96%: 23.1, 97%: 24.3, 98%: 25.9). XGBoost can therefore say how unsure it is, and honestly reports that it is quite unsure; the LLM gives no range at all. Its compressed scores look confident but carry an average error of 17.8.

Prompt sensitivity audit. A single example number in the prompt (score 50) made Mistral cluster most answers at 55–70; replacing it with placeholders spread them out. Together with the 13.8-point rewording effect and 8.4-point framing effect, the LLM's errors reflect how the question is asked as well as the economy itself, a variability the explainable model does not have.

7  Limitations and Conclusion

The most important limitation is that the score is a GDP-per-capita proxy and that XGBoost and SHAP describe association: a plan that raises the predicted score may not raise real income, and the debt result shows why. Testing that claim would need a backtest: plans made from 2014–2018 data, compared with what countries actually achieved. Other limits: 52 countries; one small LLM, which may also have memorised GDP figures; realism and direction referees we designed ourselves; energy estimated rather than measured; and Mistral at temperature 0 was still not fully repeatable. Conclusion: XGBoost + SHAP does better at scoring countries (accurate, fair, consistent, with stated uncertainty) and at cost; the LLM does better at proposing plans that follow economic logic, but its plans are often unrealistic, especially for poor countries. The two systems fail in different, complementary ways.
